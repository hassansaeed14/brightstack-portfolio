01 — The research question

Smart home apps rarely fail because a control is missing — they fail because every control is visible at once. The research question here was narrower: what's the smallest set of controls a household actually touches daily, and can everything else move one tap deeper without feeling hidden?

Pain point

Every device, every time

Existing panels list every paired device on one screen regardless of room or relevance, so finding "the hallway light" means scanning past thirty other things first.

"I just wanted the porch light. I ended up in the thermostat schedule."
Pain point

No sense of current state at a glance

Flat icon grids don't visually distinguish "on" from "off" until you're close enough to read a small status label — a problem specifically for a panel mounted at a distance.

"From across the room I genuinely can't tell what's still running."
Design principle

Room-first, not device-first

Devices are grouped and filtered by room tab, so the panel only ever shows what's relevant to where the household actually is.

Design principle

State readable from across a room

"On" devices get a colored glow and background tint, not just a label — legible at a glance, the way a real light switch is.

02 — From cluttered to calm

The first structural pass listed every device flatly. The room-tab structure that replaced it is the single decision that came directly out of the research.

Rejected structure

Every device, one flat grid — no room context, no way to tell relevant from irrelevant at a glance.

→
Adopted structure

Room tabs filter the device grid down to 4-6 relevant tiles at a time. "On" state uses a glow, not just a label, so it reads from across the room. Try it in the working panel below.

04 — Usability notes

Observations from walking five participants through the panel on an actual wall-mounted tablet, framed as what changed because of what was seen.

01
The glow state was read correctly from six feet away

All five participants could identify which devices were on without approaching the tablet, once "on" carried a colored glow and tinted background rather than a text label alone — directly validating the research's core complaint.

02
Room tabs needed to persist the previous selection

Two participants expected the panel to reopen on whichever room they'd left it on, not default back to "Living room" — the panel now remembers the last active tab between sessions.

03
The temperature dial's tap targets needed to be bigger, not the dial itself

The +/− buttons were originally smaller and closer together; participants missed them at an arm's length from a wall mount. Enlarging just the buttons (not the whole dial) fixed it without disrupting the layout.
